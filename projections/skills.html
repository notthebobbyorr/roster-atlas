Model structure & feature importance

From process skills to fantasy grades

Surface outcomes at each level → MLB equivalencies + level-share ballast → stable seasonal lines → inferred or regressed process skills → prior-three-season skill estimate → age adjustment → category rates → park scenario → standard workload → fixed-reference SD grades.

Each surface stat is translated at its actual level before seasons are combined. Missing-season ballast is allocated by the player’s actual playing-time shares across levels. Ballast uses MLB-average surface rates with the same level penalties. Historical rolling tests select a season-size floor and a minimum regression amount: ballast = max(floor − observed PA/TBF, minimum regression). Full seasons therefore receive regression too. Ballast never counts as observed evidence.

Steal translations use same-season level transitions so development is not included in the level penalty and then applied again as aging. Additional speed regression is selected on chronological MiLB-to-MLB transitions. Stable seasonal lines infer minor-league process KPIs; measured MLB skills receive separately validated shrinkage toward MLB skill baselines. Missing physical measurements use explicit MLB priors with uncertainty rather than invented tracking data. The three preceding calendar seasons receive 1/2/3 recency weights after stabilization; missing seasons are not compressed or filled as observed seasons.

For inferred MiLB profiles, the category baseline remains anchored to the stable surface equivalency. The process model supplies the age delta, not a second independent estimate that can contradict the surface line. Mixed histories blend that anchor with the independent MLB process model according to their minor-league share and the amount of independently measured MLB skill evidence. As measured MLB samples grow, the independent process model receives more weight; the transition uses the already validated skill-regression exposure. A separate age model adjusts that initial skill estimate, using only completed MLB target seasons. Unknown birth dates receive no age adjustment. Park effects come after the skill and category forecast. MLB-average inputs, translation factors, regression selection, skill calibration and age fits all stop before the forecast year. Added minor profiles do not redefine the league run environment or SD reference.

Category mappings are exposure-weighted ridge regressions fitted to regressed, park-neutral MLB outcome lines. Training labels receive the existing PA/TBF ballast with MLB priors from the preceding three seasons; event counts and AB/IP denominators are stabilized together. Coefficient-training and error-evaluation seasons require at least 200 actual AB for hitters or 40 actual IP for pitchers. Regression ballast does not count toward eligibility. These cutoffs do not remove sparse players from the forecast population or redefine the frozen SD reference. Observed samples determine fitting weights. Validation and regularization selection still score actual, unregressed subsequent MLB outcomes. Their features are centered at training medians and divided by training standard deviations. Stabilized profiles require at least 20 actual recent PA/TBF and half of each component’s features. Estimated-input ranges include proxy residuals and missing-physical-prior uncertainty; they are sensitivity bands, not calibrated confidence intervals.

How the categories are assembled

  • Power: modeled HR per AB × 600 AB.
  • AVG: (1 − strikeouts per AB) × hits per contacted AB, with hits at least as high as HR. Contact risk and contact quality are modeled separately.
  • Speed: opportunities per AB × forecast takeoff rate × pooled prior-season steal-success rate × 600 AB. Takeoff is a separate forecast, so the opportunity-model weights below are only part of Speed.
  • K: modeled strikeouts per IP × role workload.
  • ERA: 9 × modeled earned runs per IP. WHIP: modeled hits per IP + walks per IP. Park adjustments use HR and non-HR-hit components.

Park scenarios combine 50% selected home park and 50% neutral road. Workloads are 600 AB / 175 SP IP / 60 RP IP / 100 swingman IP. Contribution grades use excess hits, earned runs prevented, and baserunners prevented for the ratio categories. Lower ERA and WHIP become higher grades. Sum gives the three final grades equal weight.

Reading the fitted feature weights

These are actual process-model coefficients. Surface anchors also affect inferred MiLB projections, so these weights are not a complete decomposition of those final projections. Coefficients are ranked by absolute size within each component. A signed coefficient is the change in the component rate for a +1 training SD change in that input, holding the others fixed. The bar is that feature’s share of the sum of absolute coefficients in this component.

These shares describe model weights, not causal importance, explained variance, or a player’s personal contribution. Correlated features can share weight or have surprising signs. Shares cannot be compared across components or added into a final AVG, WHIP, or Speed importance score. Positive means a higher component rate: for strikeouts per AB, hits allowed, walks allowed, and earned runs allowed, that is unfavorable.

Feature weights by input season

Here n is the most recent completed input season, not the forecast season. With all three seasonal inputs present, n receives 50%, n−1 receives 33.3%, and n−2 receives 16.7%. These are fixed recency weights, not separately learned yearly importances. The model fits one coefficient per combined skill; each year’s rate effect below is that coefficient multiplied by its recency weight.

Each year cell shows the signed component-rate effect and its share of total absolute feature-year weight. The perturbation is a change in that stabilized seasonal skill equal to one training SD of the combined forecast input—not one SD of that individual season and not one dashboard grade SD. Other seasonal inputs are held fixed. Missing seasonal inputs are excluded and remaining weights are renormalized per feature. Within-season regression, inferred surface anchors, age adjustments, clipping and park effects can change the response to raw observations; this is a breakdown of the linear process mapping, not a complete player attribution.

How to interpret this model

600 AB for hitters · 175 IP for starters · 60 IP for relievers · 100 IP for swingmen. These are comparison workloads, not forecasts of health or playing time. Role overrides change workload only.

Quality compares rates. Contribution includes volume: excess hits for AVG, earned runs prevented for ERA, baserunners prevented for WHIP, and category totals for HR, SB and K. The pitcher scale is shared across all three roles. Reference: preseason-eligible player profiles from held-out 2025/2026 forecasts, with at least 100 AB or 30 IP in the prior season. The scale stays fixed when filtering players, changing parks or overriding workload.

Each park scenario is 50% selected home park and 50% neutral road. Player contact shape and batting side inform separate HR and non-HR-hit effects; these feed AVG, ERA and WHIP. SB, K and walks have no park effect in this version. Park-factor estimation remains an approximation, not a causal guarantee.

Speed means SB contribution. It combines forecast takeoff rate, modeled opportunities and a pooled prior-season steal-success rate. It is not a pure running-speed measurement. R, RBI, wins and saves are deferred.

Outcome ranges show the middle 80% of held-out residuals translated to grade units. They include season-to-season luck and are not confidence intervals for true skill. Small changes in point grades should not be treated as certain differences.

Roster context is the configured October 3, 2026 snapshot. Players without a projected MLB role use neutral park when Current home park is selected. Choose a named park to explore a move. Added minor-league profiles do not change the fixed MLB reference or run environment. Profiles without usable 2024–2026 inputs remain unavailable. Missing skills remain unavailable.

Held-out category validation

2025 and 2026 predictions use only earlier training outcomes. Negative RMSE difference means the skill model beat a simple regressed-rate baseline. A positive difference is worse; standardizing the output does not change that.
